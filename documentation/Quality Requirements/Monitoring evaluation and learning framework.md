This framework defines how Namaa should design a proportionate monitoring, evaluation, and learning system once a specific programme and pilot are proposed. It does not prove need, effectiveness, impact, safety, inclusion, or implementation readiness. Programme-specific outcomes, instruments, targets, thresholds, and timing remain to be developed and approved by qualified specialists.

## 2. Purpose

At pilot stage, MEL should answer four practical questions:

1. Is the programme being delivered safely and as intended?
2. Who can access, start, participate regularly, complete, and benefit—and who cannot?
3. Is there credible evidence of change in the specific knowledge or competence the module actually teaches?
4. Does the evidence justify continuing, adapting, pausing, stopping, or preparing a stronger test?

MEL is not a mechanism for marketing claims. A small early pilot should support safe management and learning. It should not be designed or described as proof of long-term impact on children's lives.

## 3. Definitions and separation of functions

| Function | Purpose | Typical output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monitoring | Regularly tracks delivery, participation, quality, safety, access, and changing context | Session and programme records, alerts, routine reviews |
| Evaluation | Systematically and impartially assesses the merit, results, limitations, and lessons of a defined programme or pilot | Evaluation questions, analysis, findings, conclusions, recommendations |
| Learning | Converts evidence, feedback, complaints, incidents, and experience into controlled decisions and improvements | Continue/adapt/pause/stop decision, change record, revised design |

Routine monitoring, evaluation or research, ordinary feedback, service complaints, safeguarding concerns, and data incidents are not interchangeable. They require different purposes, channels, access rights, response competencies, and records.

## 5. Evaluation approach for an early pilot

The OECD-DAC criteria should be applied according to the pilot's maturity and evidence:

| Criterion | Appropriate pilot question | Claim limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relevance | Does the module address a clearly defined need and matter to the intended children and caregivers? | One group or location does not establish relevance across Syria. |
| Coherence | Does the module complement local education and support services, policy, and referral systems without harmful duplication? | Desk mapping alone does not prove current local fit. |
| Effectiveness | Were the defined activities delivered and were the intended proximal outcomes observed for different participants? | Observed change does not prove causation without an adequate design. |
| Efficiency | Were time, expertise, materials, technology, and other resources used reasonably compared with feasible alternatives of similar quality? | Low cost is not efficiency if quality, safety, access, or outcomes are inadequate. |
| Impact | What early positive, negative, intended, or unintended higher-level signals appear? | A small uncontrolled pilot must not claim attributable long-term impact. |
| Sustainability | What conditions would be needed to maintain delivery, capability, access, and benefits? | Endline data cannot establish that benefits will last. |

Relevance, coherence, effectiveness, and feasibility or efficiency are the main pilot lenses. Sustainability should be treated as a conditions analysis. Impact should be used to identify early signals and unintended effects, not to manufacture a causal claim.

## 6. Working theory of change

The following is a draft structure, not a validated causal model.

### Preconditions and inputs

- one specialist-approved programme module with a controlled version;
- trained and practically assessed teachers or facilitators;
- understandable, culturally reviewed, accessible Arabic materials by default, with a target language used where that language is itself taught and the specialist-approved design requires it;
- an accountable local host, appropriate setting, and required permissions;
- adopted safeguarding, complaints, referral, ethics, and data controls;
- specialist coaching, supervision, and adequate operational resources; and
- a MEL plan using tools suitable for the objective, age, language, disability-related needs, and context.

### Activities

- safe and transparent participant information and enrolment;
- appropriate permission for programme participation and, separately, for non-routine evidence activity;
- baseline only where justified;
- regular structured learning and support sessions;
- learner-centred and accessible activities, including play-based methods where appropriate;
- formative assessment and teaching adaptation;
- supervision and coaching of teachers or facilitators;
- accessible feedback and complaints; and
- safe recognition, reporting, and referral of safeguarding concerns.

### Outputs

- planned and actually delivered sessions;
- children who were informed, eligible, enrolled, started, participated, and completed;
- teachers or facilitators trained, assessed, authorised, supervised, and supported;
- approved materials and accessibility formats used;
- accommodations requested and provided;
- feedback and complaints received and addressed; and
- referrals made through approved routes, reported only in safe aggregate form for MEL.

### Proximal outcomes

- change in the precise knowledge, skill, or competence taught by the module;
- perceived relevance, safety, accessibility, and usefulness;
- adequate implementation fidelity and facilitator competence;
- workable inclusion and accommodations.

Participation and completion are process or engagement evidence, not substitutes for learning or benefit. Safety is a continuously monitored condition and gate, not a positive programme outcome. The risk-benefit judgement must consider the severity, recurrence, exposure, reporting accessibility, detection limits, and learning from all incidents, including those that have been administratively closed.

### Later hypotheses

Depending on the selected programme, later inquiry may examine retention of learning, application in practice, continued education, or connection to another appropriate pathway. These are open hypotheses and should be measured only when the programme theory, timeframe, permissions, and follow-up feasibility justify them.

### Long-term aspiration

Namaa aspires over time to make safe additional learning and support opportunities accessible to as many children in need in Syria as responsible capacity allows and thereby contribute to stronger capabilities, agency, future prospects, quality of life, and a better future for Syrian society. This is a strategic aspiration and contribution model. A small pilot cannot attribute these long-term outcomes to Namaa or claim to be a complete solution to Syria's educational or societal challenges.

### External assumptions

Security, displacement, poverty, transport, child labour, family responsibilities, school changes, health, infrastructure, power, connectivity, availability of qualified people, and functioning local services may affect participation and outcomes independently of Namaa. These factors must be reported where relevant rather than treated as programme failure or success without analysis.

## 7. Required results chain and MEL matrix

Before a pilot, specialists must convert the working theory into a programme-specific results chain. Every result and indicator must have a defined use. Data should not be collected merely because it may be interesting.

The controlled MEL matrix must include:

| Required field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Result or question | The exact output, outcome, risk, or decision to be understood |
| Indicator | Observable measure; not a vague ambition |
| Definition and calculation | Unit, numerator, denominator, inclusion and exclusion rules where applicable |
| Source and tool | Where and how the information is obtained |
| Timing or frequency | Event- or programme-based schedule justified by the intended use |
| Responsible role | Collection, quality check, analysis, safeguarding escalation, and decision ownership |
| Disaggregation | Only characteristics needed to assess equity or programme purpose |
| Accessibility and accommodation | How participants with different communication or functional needs can take part fairly |
| Data and ethics classification | Identifiability, sensitivity, lawful and ethical basis, access, retention, and review decision |
| Quality risks | Missingness, bias, tool validity, translation, observer effects, attrition, small groups, or burden |
| Interpretation limit | What the indicator cannot show |
| Decision rule | What finding or condition triggers review, adaptation, pause, or escalation |

No numeric success target should be added until the programme, cohort, instrument, baseline or plausible reference, and decision use are defined by qualified specialists. Where a threshold depends on baseline information, the method for setting it must be approved in advance. Success and decision rules must be frozen before outcome data are examined; any later change must be versioned and treated as an adaptation. Non-numeric safety conditions apply immediately and are never adjusted to make results appear acceptable.

### 7.1 Sampling and evaluability plan

Before collection, the programme must document who the evidence is intended to describe, the eligible population, recruitment route, inclusion and exclusion rules, and whether the design uses a census or sample. The plan must address selection bias, likely missingness and attrition, and how people who decline or leave will remain visible without coercive follow-up.

For quantitative questions, specialists must document the precision, power, minimum meaningful change, or other proportionate rationale needed for the intended inference; this requirement does not authorise invented targets. For qualitative questions, the plan must justify participant selection, diversity of perspectives, and an appropriate stopping or saturation rationale. Any comparison group, historical reference, or contribution analysis requires a pre-specified purpose, feasibility, ethical justification, and rival-explanation strategy. An evaluability check must confirm that the results chain is clear, measures are fit for the decision, implementation and context can be described, and the available design can support the planned claim.

## 8. Indicator architecture

### 8.1 Participation and participant flow

The system should distinguish, where safe and useful, the sequence:

> informed or reached -> assessed against transparent eligibility -> offered a place -> appropriately enrolled -> started -> participated -> completed -> followed up or connected onward, where relevant.

Counts and percentages require clear denominators. Reasons for non-start, absence, withdrawal, and loss to follow-up should be captured only through voluntary, proportionate, non-punitive methods. People who leave must not disappear from interpretation.

Participation and completion may indicate access or engagement. They must not be interpreted as learning, effectiveness, or benefit without separate outcome evidence.

### 8.2 Delivery and implementation fidelity

Possible measures include planned versus delivered sessions, dose or exposure, cancellations and reasons, use of the approved version, use of key teaching practices, adherence to professional boundaries, supervision, coaching, and authorised adaptations. Specialists must define a short observation or fidelity rubric and what variation is acceptable.

Fidelity is not blind compliance. A justified adaptation may improve quality, but it must be documented so that results are not attributed to a programme different from the one actually delivered.

### 8.3 Trainer and facilitator development

The framework should distinguish attendance, completion, demonstrated competence, programme-specific authorisation, observed initial delivery, continuing coaching, confidence, workload, wellbeing, and retention. A self-reported confidence increase is not proof of teaching competence.

### 8.4 Learning and other proximal outcomes

The responsible specialists must select or develop measures tightly aligned with what was taught and the decisions to be made. Baseline and endline should use comparable methods and accommodations. Assessment must be fair, non-threatening, understandable, age-appropriate, linguistically suitable, confidential, and accessible.

Possible evidence includes structured task performance, age-appropriate demonstration, formative assessment, work samples, observation, or validated measures where suitable. Health, psychosocial, disability, or protection outcomes require the relevant specialist competence and may trigger a higher ethical review.

### 8.5 Safety and unintended effects

The system should monitor safeguarding concerns, adverse events, near misses, distress or burden caused by the programme or measurement, data incidents, unsafe deviations, failures of required staffing or venue controls, and whether reporting and referral remain functional. Sensitive case details belong in protected systems, not the MEL dataset.

No report volume is an automatic safety metric. A fall in reports may reflect improvement, fear, inaccessibility, lack of awareness, or under-reporting.

### 8.6 Equity, inclusion, and accommodations

Access, start, participation, completion, attrition, outcomes, feedback, and burden should be compared across only those characteristics needed to answer a justified equity question. The system should also record accommodations requested, provided, unavailable, and effective, without exposing individuals.

Disability inclusion should combine accessible design with individual accommodations. Namaa should involve persons with disabilities, children with disabilities, caregivers, and organisations of persons with disabilities in suitable ways. It must not use a crude label or collect functional data unless a valid purpose, suitable method, competent team, safeguards, and response capability exist. Small groups or cells must be suppressed or combined only where this does not conceal a material inequity.

### 8.7 Feedback, complaints, and perceived usefulness

Children and caregivers should have voluntary, accessible ways to describe relevance, safety, accessibility, burden, and usefulness. Feedback tools must not be the sole complaints or safeguarding channel. People must understand the difference and know how to switch to the protected route.

### 8.8 Efficiency and resource use

Resource analysis may include staff and volunteer time, specialist support, materials, translation, accessibility, transport, devices, connectivity, supervision, safeguarding, and data work. Costs per participant or completer are meaningful only with clear definitions and quality context. Comparison should be with a realistic alternative capable of delivering comparable safety and quality.

## 9. Measurement stages

Timing must follow the programme and outcome rather than a generic calendar.

### Before start

- finalise the theory of change, MEL questions, matrix, definitions, tools, analysis plan, and claim boundaries;
- complete the ethics and safeguarding triage and any required independent review;
- complete the data-management and deletion plan;
- translate and test comprehension, cultural suitability, burden, accessibility, and safe use of instruments without using children before the required approval;
- train and assess data collectors; and
- collect a baseline only after the relevant programme, ethical, permission, consent or assent, and data gates are passed.

### During each relevant delivery event

Collect the minimum justified information on participation, delivery, required accommodations, significant deviations, immediate safety, and voluntary short feedback. Safeguarding information must move immediately to the safeguarding route, not wait for routine analysis.

### During the pilot

The programme-specific plan must define a regular quality and learning review and, where useful, a formal midpoint decision. Formative learning information should support teaching. Material changes to the programme or instrument require version control and an assessment of whether earlier and later data remain comparable.

### Endline

Where a baseline exists, use a sufficiently comparable tool, administration method, language, conditions, and accommodations. Assess the defined proximal outcomes, implementation, accessibility, burden, unintended effects, facilitator experience, feedback, and data quality. Missing data and attrition must be visible.

### Follow-up

Follow-up is used only if the theory of change predicts a meaningful later result and the added contact and data burden are justified. The timing must be selected by specialists based on the expected outcome. Permission for later contact and the minimum contact data must be handled separately.

## 10. Ethical evidence gate

Every proposed evidence activity—whether labelled monitoring, evaluation, research, consultation, user testing, observation, or data collection—requires a documented ethics and risk triage before it starts.

The triage must consider:

- whether children participate directly or can be identified;
- age, capacity, language, disability, dependence, power imbalance, exclusion, and humanitarian context;
- sensitive education, health, disability, protection, family, location, image, audio, biometric, or digital information;
- possible distress, disclosure, stigma, retaliation, exclusion, coercion, or loss of services;
- experimental assignment, comparison groups, withholding, incentives, or deception;
- recording, remote contact, third-party platforms, cross-border access, automated analysis, or AI;
- collector competence, referral capability, privacy, security, retention, deletion, and reporting limits; and
- whether the result could affect an individual child's access, status, treatment, or future.

As a proposed Namaa control, independent ethical review or an independent documented ethics determination is required before non-routine evidence activity involving children, sensitive data, intrusive methods, experimental allocation, material power imbalance, or elevated safeguarding or data risk. The competent reviewing body or qualified independent reviewer has not been identified. Applicable local law, authority, professional, partner, and funder requirements remain **not verified**.

Routine operational monitoring may receive a proportionate review where it is limited, necessary, low-risk, and non-intrusive. Calling an activity “monitoring” does not exempt it from safeguarding, data protection, ethical design, or local requirements.

Synthetic or adult-reviewed fictional data must be used for staff training and technology demonstrations. A narrowly scoped cognitive interview, comprehension check, accessibility test, or instrument pilot with children from the intended age or disability group may be necessary for a valid and usable instrument, but it is itself a child-facing evidence activity. It may occur only after a specific ethics determination or review, safeguarding and referral arrangements, appropriate caregiver permission and child assent, a minimal data plan, trained personnel, and written authorisation. Real child data must never be used in the public repository.

## 11. Permission, consent, assent, and voluntary participation

Programme participation, required operational records, optional feedback, evaluation or research, images or recordings, future contact, and secondary data use must be explained and decided separately where appropriate. Access to the learning programme must not depend on agreeing to optional research, publicity, or non-essential data collection.

For non-routine evidence involving a child, Namaa should normally obtain informed permission from the legally appropriate caregiver and age-, capacity-, language-, and communication-appropriate assent from the child. A qualified legal and ethics review must document any justified alternative. A caregiver's permission does not erase the child's dissent, distress, or wish to stop except where a competent process establishes a compelling and lawful reason.

Information must explain the purpose, procedures, expected duration, possible benefits and risks, voluntariness, alternatives, data use, access, retention, reporting, withdrawal without disadvantage, contact routes, and limits of confidentiality. Consent and assent are ongoing processes, not signatures alone. Communication and response methods must be accessible.

A disclosure made during MEL must be handled through the safeguarding procedure. Researchers or data collectors must not promise that a serious safety concern will remain secret.

## 12. Data management and protection

Every data field must be linked to a prior question or operational necessity. The data plan must define controller and processor roles, lawful and ethical basis, collection method, identifiers, pseudonymisation, access, storage, transfer, security, backup, incident response, retention, deletion, and reporting.

Use participant codes rather than names where possible. Store any re-identification key separately with narrower access. Do not place safeguarding case details, free-text disclosures, health records, images, contact lists, or sensitive child-level data in ordinary MEL files. Do not upload identifiable or sensitive information to AI systems without a separate approved legal, ethical, safeguarding, security, and technical basis.

Cross-border access between Germany, Syria, and service providers requires a separate data-protection assessment. Aggregation does not guarantee anonymity in a small cohort; reports must consider indirect identification and suppress unsafe detail.

## 13. Data quality and analysis

The MEL Lead must define proportionate checks for completeness, consistency, tool administration, translation, accessibility, duplicate records, missing values, outliers, changes in instruments, and secure correction. Original and corrected values must remain traceable in the controlled system.

Analysis must:

- use the pre-defined questions and plan or explain deviations;
- show denominators, participant flow, missing data, attrition, and exclusions;
- compare groups only when sample size, ethics, data quality, and interpretation justify it;
- report negative, null, unequal, and unintended findings as well as positive results;
- distinguish participant report, observation, administrative record, and assessed outcome;
- account for context and plausible alternative explanations;
- disclose conflicts of interest and the degree of evaluator independence; and
- state limits on reliability, validity, transferability, and causality.

Routine delivery teams may collect process data after training and supervision. Programme outcome analysis should be quality-checked by a competent person who was not responsible for routine delivery. Scaling, general-effectiveness, or impact claims require stronger independence and evidence than internal pilot monitoring.

## 14. Learning and decision cycle

Evidence must lead to a recorded decision, not only a report.

1. **Immediate escalation:** safeguarding, serious safety, data, or professional-boundary concerns use their specialist route immediately.
2. **Routine review:** authorised staff examine participation, fidelity, accommodations, workload, feedback, data quality, and open actions at the frequency defined in the pilot plan.
3. **Mid-course decision where appropriate:** continue unchanged, make a controlled adaptation, pause, or stop.
4. **End-of-pilot sense-making:** specialists, local implementers, safeguarding and MEL roles interpret the evidence together. Children and caregivers receive safe, accessible opportunities to comment on findings where ethically appropriate.
5. **Management decision:** Namaa leadership records the evidence, limitations, option, decision, conditions, owner, and next review.
6. **Feedback to participants:** key findings and resulting changes are shared in an understandable and accessible form, subject to privacy and safety.

## 15. Stop, pause, adapt, continue, and scale rules

### Hard pre-start stop

No child-related evidence activity may start while any required programme approval, ethical determination, safeguarding and referral route, trained data collector, instrument testing that can be completed without children, consent or assent process, data-management plan, permission, or competent supervision is missing. Any remaining child-facing comprehension, accessibility, or validity testing requires its own bounded authorisation before the child participates.

### Immediate pause or stop during activity

Pause and escalate when there is actual or credible risk of serious or unmanaged harm; coercion or retaliation; a material confidentiality or data breach; prohibited AI or data use; repeated substantial distress caused by the programme or instrument; loss of a functioning safeguarding or referral route; an unsafe change in context; or instruction from the accountable safeguarding, ethics, local, or programme authority.

Resumption is prohibited until the incident and risk have been assessed, required corrective actions are verified, safeguarding, data, ethics, and programme controls are updated, any required independent or authority re-review is complete, and affected people receive appropriate information or support. Namaa project leadership, as the default gate owner, must then issue a written restart decision for the exact scope after all required specialist and local sign-offs, written safeguarding clearance from the Senior Accountable Officer, and written concurrence from the Safeguarding Lead. A different single decision-maker may act only where the governing body has delegated that exact decision in writing. Where an ethics review was required for the activity or is triggered by the incident or proposed change, the required independent ethics re-review must also be complete. If a role-holder is implicated or conflicted, the formally designated substitute acts for that role; an unresolved child-safety barrier cannot be overridden.

### Adapt and re-review

Adaptation is required where evidence shows material participation or outcome differences, inaccessible information or tools, unmet accommodations, weak delivery fidelity, excessive burden, misunderstanding, unsafe or impractical processes, facilitator overload, or missingness and attrition that undermine interpretation. The change must be versioned and its effect on comparability assessed.

### Limited continuation

Continuation can be considered only where the safeguarding system is functioning, the full risk-benefit assessment—including resolved incidents and detection limits—is acceptable, delivery and data quality meet criteria defined before outcome data are examined, the programme is feasible for the responsible people and setting, and the outcome pattern is consistent with the theory of change alongside adequate implementation evidence. “Contribution” should be claimed only where a defined contribution analysis and plausible rival explanations have been assessed. Any conditions and uncertainties remain explicit.

### Replication or scale

One pilot is not sufficient by itself. Replication or scale requires the [NQ-G5 gate](Programme%20quality%20framework.md), current local readiness, adequate capacity and permissions, repeated or stronger evidence, and greater independent scrutiny as the claim and exposure grow.

## 16. Permitted and prohibited claims

Subject to sound data and transparent limitations, Namaa may report:

- how many people were informed, eligible, enrolled, started, participated, and completed;
- what was delivered, by whom, and how closely it matched the approved design;
- what accommodations were requested and provided;
- observed baseline-to-endline change on a named measure closely linked to taught content;
- participant, caregiver, and facilitator feedback;
- group differences that can be reported safely and credibly;
- incidents, unintended effects, burden, missingness, attrition, and context; and
- the resulting continue, adapt, pause, stop, or further-study decision.

Without a suitable comparison or counterfactual strategy and adequate methods, Namaa must not say that it **caused** the observed change. It must not generalise from one cohort or place to all children in Syria; describe long-term benefits as established from an endline; claim formal impact, effectiveness, inclusion, safety, or sustainability beyond the evidence; omit non-completers; or hide changes in design or instruments.

The OECD impact criterion asks what significant difference an intervention makes. It is not itself evidence that Namaa made that difference.

## 17. Minimum controlled MEL package before a pilot

1. programme-specific theory of change and assumptions or risk register;
2. MEL questions, evaluability and sampling plan, matrix, and indicator reference sheets;
3. ethics triage and review decision, with consent and assent materials where applicable;
4. data-management, access, transfer, retention, deletion, and incident plan;
5. translated and accessibility-tested instruments with version control;
6. participant-flow, session, fidelity, accommodation, supervision, and deviation records;
7. separate feedback, complaint, safeguarding, and data-incident routes and registers;
8. pre-specified analysis and reporting plan;
9. change log and continue, adapt, pause, or stop decision record; and
10. an accessible learning brief that includes limitations and unintended findings.
