# Namaa Problem Landscape and Evidence

- **Document status:** Draft evidence synthesis
- **Version:** 0.1
- **Underlying desk research:** July 2026
- **Compilation date:** 1 August 2026
- **Responsible role:** Namaa project leadership
- **Project phase:** Exploration and concept development
- **Verification boundary:** This document consolidates previously reviewed sources. It is not a new live source review.
- **Related decision record:** [Project dossier](../core/Project%20dossier.md)

## Purpose and evidence boundary

The reviewed evidence establishes both a severe education problem in Syria and a substantial existing provider landscape. It does not establish that educational programmes are generally absent, that every location has the same needs, or that Namaa's proposed model is already the correct response.

The strongest evidence-compatible concern is discontinuity across a support pathway:

> offer available -> family informed -> child admitted -> regular participation -> completion -> transition and retention

The reviewed public sources document activities and reach but do not provide a current local picture of capacity, waiting lists, participation intensity, completion, transition, retention, or learning outcomes. Namaa's focus on regular and measurable support pathways is therefore a project working premise consistent with the evidence, not a verified sector-wide finding.

## Reach and continuity constraints

In the first quarter of 2026, UNICEF-supported education activity was reported in 13 of 14 governorates, 139 of 270 subdistricts, and 632 of 8,364 recorded communities. These figures cover UNICEF-supported activity only and do not prove that communities outside this footprint had no other services. [UNICEF education snapshot Q1 2026](https://www.unicef.org/syria/media/23576/file/Syria-humanitarian-response-snapshot-education-Jan-Mar-2026.pdf)

Reported reach varies substantially in intensity. In 2025, 3.4 million children were counted through formal education support or receipt of materials, while 313,000 participated in non-formal education. In the first quarter of 2026, 95,359 children participated in remedial, catch-up, or bridging activities, while 19,786 were supported in accessing formal education. Supported access is not a confirmed transition, and material distribution is not evidence of regular pedagogical support. Reporting categories may overlap. [UNICEF education snapshot 2025](https://www.unicef.org/syria/media/22661/file/Syria-humanitarian-response-snapshot-education-Jan-Dec-2025.pdf); [UNICEF Q1 2026](https://www.unicef.org/media/180531/file/Syrian-Arab-Republic-Humanitarian-Situation-Report-No.1%2C31-March-2026.pdf.pdf)

The public UNICEF service map is a useful starting point, but it does not establish current service availability, capacity, waiting lists, attendance frequency, completion, transition, retention, or learning outcomes. [UNICEF service mapping](https://www.unicef.org/syria/documents/unicef-programme-service-mapping-syria)

The evidence supports concern about geography, continuity, intensity, accessibility, and outcome visibility. It does not support a general claim that existing programmes fail or that a new provider is automatically required.

## Administrative constraints

Documentation gaps remain relevant, particularly for returning families. The UNHCR assessment reports civil-document problems at household level, but these percentages must not be treated as national child rates. [UNHCR Q1 2026](https://data.unhcr.org/en/documents/download/122228)

Provisional enrolment introduced for the 2025/26 school year could permit initial school access despite incomplete documents. The documents still had to be completed later, and failure could affect certification. Continued applicability in 2026/27 and consistent local implementation were not verified in the earlier review. [UNHCR education information](https://help.unhcr.org/syria/where-to-find-help/education/)

Recognition of education completed abroad may involve prior records, authentication, education directorates, placement, equivalence procedures, or examinations. Public information did not establish processing times or consistent regional practice. A March 2026 ministry announcement on supplementary examinations also appeared inconsistent with parts of the available UNHCR guidance; the currently controlling rule and local application were not verified. [Ministry announcement via SANA](https://sana.sy/education/2425127/); [UNHCR education information](https://help.unhcr.org/syria/where-to-find-help/education/)

DG ECHO additionally describes unpredictable administrative restrictions and registration or re-registration requirements affecting humanitarian organisations. Public evidence does not quantify education-specific delays. [DG ECHO Syria Humanitarian Implementation Plan 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/echo/files/funding/hip2026/echo_syr_bud_2026_91000_v2.pdf)

The Local Education Group and new statistical cooperation indicate efforts to improve coordination and evidence. They are consistent with continuing development of coordination and data systems; this is an inference rather than a direct finding of the two announcements. [Local Education Group](https://sana.sy/en/education/2327811/); [Statistical cooperation](https://sana.sy/en/education/2315115/)

## Technology and general operating constraints

The previously reviewed sources document combinations of electricity, connectivity, security, infrastructure, staffing, poverty, displacement, and funding constraints. [World Bank electricity programme](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2025/06/25/syria-world-bank-us-146-million-grant-to-improve-electricity-supply-and-support-sector-development); [UN Syria analysis](https://syria.un.org/en/download/180806/291750); [UNICEF appeal](https://www.unicef.org/appeals/syria)

Device availability for intended Namaa users was not directly verified and remains an implementation assumption. No reviewed source established organisation-specific cyber incidents or data breaches.

The main technology-related evidence gap concerns visibility and continuity across organisations. Public sources do not expose a current shared picture of capacity, waiting lists, referrals, attendance, progress, and transitions. This does not prove that organisations have no internal systems.

For Namaa, offline-capable, low-bandwidth, device-light, accessible, facilitator-led, and data-minimising design is a prudent implication of the documented environment. It is not evidence that a digital or AI solution is itself needed. Technology cannot replace safe spaces, qualified professionals, safeguarding, transport, school capacity, or economic support.

## Relationship to Namaa's working problem

The authoritative working problem and target-group decision are maintained in the [Project dossier](../core/Project%20dossier.md), not in this evidence module. The reviewed evidence is compatible with concern about unreliable or discontinuous support pathways, but it does not verify their scale, location, causes, or the precise Namaa service gap.

Evidence is comparatively stronger for out-of-school children, returnees, children with disabilities, and prolonged education interruptions. Puberty-specific education and support needs remain less thoroughly researched in the reviewed source set. This evidence-strength statement does not change or prioritise the target-group decision.

## What the evidence does not establish

The reviewed material does not establish:

- a selected Namaa location;
- current local places, waiting lists, refusal reasons, or processing times;
- the frequency and duration of participation in existing services;
- completion, transition, retention, or learning-outcome rates;
- which barrier is dominant for which subgroup;
- that sport, coding, health literacy, or life-skills modules are the highest-priority intervention;
- that technology or AI is necessary;
- a suitable first age group or cohort;
- a confirmed implementing organisation or authority;
- approval, funding, implementation readiness, or likely impact;
- that Namaa's proposed activities would add value rather than duplicate existing provision.

## Open evidence questions

1. Which target-group needs should receive priority within the broad Namaa concept?
2. What current support capacity, participation intensity, completion, transition, and retention data exist beyond public reports?
3. Which administrative route currently applies to enrolment, placement, foreign records, certification, and disability-related access?
4. Which existing organisations provide sustained rather than one-off support to each selected group?
5. What programme content and delivery method should qualified specialists recommend?
6. What referral pathways exist for education, disability support, health, psychosocial support, and safeguarding?
7. What electricity, connectivity, device, language, and accessibility conditions must any future tool accommodate?
8. Would an AI-assisted process outperform a simpler non-AI process in quality, access, or facilitator workload?
9. What evidence would demonstrate Namaa's additional value without treating missing public data as proof of a service gap?

These questions do not create a standalone local-validation project in the current phase. They identify facts that must be resolved when a selected programme, partner, location, technology, pilot, or funding route makes them decision-relevant.
